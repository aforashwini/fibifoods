01

The creative brief

Everything the agency makes flows from the brief. Its parts:

  • Objective · what you want to achieve
  • Target · who you are talking to
  • Insight · what drives their behaviour
  • Position · how you want to be seen
  • Emotion · what you want them to feel
  • Measurement · how you will measure it
02

Objectives

Grow revenue by X, and so on. You have to translate the objective for the agency. The logic: how do I get a target to behave differently? To grow by X, I need X people to use my product X times per month.

Common objectives

  • Awareness (e.g. a 14% increase in awareness)
  • Attitude shift
  • Perception / evaluation

Reference · the IKEA "Lamp" ad

Spike Jonze, 2002. A woman unplugs an old desk lamp and leaves it out on the curb. As mournful music plays and the rain comes down, the shots are framed so the little lamp looks almost human, and you start to feel genuinely sorry for it. Then a man walks into frame and tells you, more or less, that feeling bad for the lamp is crazy, because it has no feelings, and the new one is much better. The lesson: an ad can build real emotion toward an object and then puncture it on purpose. Emotion is something you direct, not something that just happens.

03

Goal vs. marketing objective

Two levels: the business goal (revenue up by X) and the marketing objective (the target behaves differently). Then ask the diagnostic question:

Why is the target not behaving the way we want?

Identify the shortfalls and impediments. That is what leads to the decision to advertise.

05

Research

  • Focus groups help us learn things we had not thought of.
  • Research is bad for judging an ad. It is qualitative, and consumers cannot visualise what an ad will look like, so do not listen to them on that.
  • Copy testing: use a rough version, in a naturalised environment, and people should not know what they are evaluating.

07

Targeting

Shopper vs. user

Who is making the decision? The shopper and the user are not always the same.

Targeting a new segment

When you go after a new segment, check on your current users: are they alienated? Protected? Safe from alienation?

Targeting and age

When your target customers are aging out, you have to change your target. But you cannot run two ads at once. If you need to, launch a sub-brand.

Case · Old Spice

A growing / aging customer segment is a common issue most brands face at some point. The base gets older, and everyone younger thinks the brand is uncool and for old people. You bring it back to the young by refocusing on what they want. You cannot do everything at once, so this might not sit right with the current base. The challenge is to do it without shaking the existing base too much.

08

Reach, influence and method

Results can be unexpected across different ad methods because of who is reached. Hence the funnel: just because one method can reach more people does not mean it is the right one, if it is not reaching the target audience or getting strong conversion down the funnel.

Reach+ Influence= Outcomes
Case · Coinbase

The Super Bowl spot that was just a QR code bouncing around an otherwise blank screen. It grabbed enormous attention, but the conversion funnel from there was poor: getting people from the scan to actually signing up and sticking around did not follow through. Huge reach, weak conversion.

Channels

SEO, SEM, GEO (Generative Engine Optimization).

09

Insight

It is easier to understand WHAT people think. It is harder to know WHY.

What weakly held beliefs do your customers have that you can change or influence? Look for under-leveraged insights.

People are driven by

  • Agency
  • Communion
  • Gratification

11

Repositioning

Repositioning happens when brand awareness is already high. Changing perception is hard, and much harder than building awareness. It can take a long time to change a brand reputation: you slowly dismantle one image and replace it with another over years.

Case · Skoda

As a Skoda owner yourself, you might not even know it once had a terrible reputation as a cheap, poor-quality car. That is exactly the point: the old image was dismantled and replaced so thoroughly, over so many years, that today it has effectively disappeared.

Also

Authenticity. And you will get tired of your ad before consumers do.

12

Performance vs. brand marketing

Most CMOs will go for performance marketing for quick results. But brand marketing is just as important, even if its effects are less easy to track and take time to become fully visible.

13

Working with an ad agency

Once you have hired an agency, the relationship itself is a skill. A few hard-won rules:

1. Give a good, detailed brief. The better the brief, the better the result. Define a clear box for them to work inside, not so wide that they wander, not so narrow that there is no room to create. As Ogilvy put it, "give me the freedom of a tight brief." Constraints focus creativity rather than smother it.

2. Let them be the experts. They are the creative team for a reason. Do not randomly object to casting or other creative calls. They know what they are doing, so trust the craft you hired.

3. Get out of their way. Once the box is set and the brief is clear, step back and let them work.

4. Every face is expensive. Each on-camera principal carries a session fee plus residuals and pension and health contributions (roughly 18 to 19 percent on top), and the cost recurs as the ad keeps running. More faces multiply it. You might think shooting abroad dodges this, but US union rules (SAG-AFTRA's Global Rule One) mean union performers stay on union terms wherever they shoot if the ad is for the US market. So you cannot just relocate to escape talent costs without a genuinely good reason.

5. Buy far more food than you think. For an edible-product shoot, order multiples of everything. Stylists routinely buy many times what a shot needs, because product arrives damaged, melts, or a take gets flubbed and reset. Ice cream is the worst offender, melting almost on contact with the lights, so it gets pre-scooped, frozen in batches and swapped constantly. A single shoot can burn through hundreds of cartons.

6. Lock in the face. If a person becomes the face of the brand, build exclusivity into their contract: they cannot work for competitors for X years, sometimes even after the deal ends. Build it in even when it is expensive to, because a face shared with a rival is worth far less. (The scope and duration have to stay reasonable, since courts scrutinise overly broad non-competes.)

★

In short

Brief: objective, target, insight, position, emotion, measurement.

Objectives: translate revenue into a behaviour. Awareness, attitude shift, perception.

Two levels: business goal and marketing objective. Ask why the target is not behaving.

4A: awareness, attitude, action, action repeated.

Research: good for discovery, bad for judging an ad. Copy test rough versions blind.

CDI = category development. BDI = brand development. Strategy before tactics.

Targeting: shopper vs user. Protect current users. Aging out means changing target. Sub-brand if needed (Old Spice).

Reach + influence = outcomes. Who you reach matters, not just how many (Coinbase). SEO, SEM, GEO.

Insight: what is easy, why is hard. Find under-leveraged beliefs. Drivers: agency, communion, gratification.

Positioning: frame of reference, points of parity and difference, function sold emotionally. Meaningful, ownable, authentic, durable.

Repositioning: high awareness, perception change is hard and slow (Skoda). Authenticity. You tire of the ad first.

Performance vs brand: performance is quick and trackable, brand is slower and just as important.

Working with an agency: tight brief (a clear box), let them be the experts, get out of the way. Every face is expensive and you cannot dodge it abroad. Overbuy food (ice cream melts). Lock faces out of competitors by contract.
